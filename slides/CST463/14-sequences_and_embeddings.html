Sequences

Order is data

Sequences are everywhere

  • A sequence is a list of items where the position of each one matters
  • The items can be words, measurements, letters in a gene, pages a user visited, notes
  • This lecture is mostly about words, because that is where the ideas are easiest to see. The same ideas apply to all of these

Order matters

  • The same items in a different order can mean something different, or nothing at all
  • Until now an example was a row of features or an image that we could treat on its own. In a sequence, where an item sits is part of the example
  • Sequences also have different lengths: a sentence of 10 words, a sentence of 40

Two kinds of items

Numbers

  • Sensor readings, prices, audio samples
  • Already numbers, so they can go into a model directly
  • The order is the part we must keep

Symbols

  • Words, letters in DNA, pages, notes
  • A choice from a list, not a number
  • We must first decide how to turn each one into numbers


The rest of this lecture is about symbols, and text is our main example

Predict the next item

  • The simplest sequence task: given the items so far, predict the next one
  • It fits every example so far: the next word, the next sensor reading, the next note, the next page
  • No separate labels are needed. The next item in the data is the answer

The Big Idea

Predict each item from the ones before it, using the same model at every position.

Our running sentence

  • 10 items, 7 different words. We trace this one sentence by hand through the whole lecture
  • “the” appears 3 times and is followed by cat, mat, cat. To predict what follows it, the words before it matter

Takeaway

A sequence is data where order matters, and the basic task is to predict the next item from the ones before it.

Text to numbers

A model only sees numbers

From text to tokens

  • A token is the unit we choose to cut a sequence into
  • For text, splitting on spaces is the simplest choice. It is a choice, not the only one
  • Symbols in other sequences need the same step: a letter of DNA, or a page visited, is a token

Vocabulary and ids

Token the cat sat on mat and ran
Id 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
Count 3 2 1 1 1 1 1
  • The vocabulary is the list of distinct tokens, here 7. Each gets an id, its position in the list
  • The sentence becomes a list of ids: [0, 1, 2, 3, 0, 4, 5, 0, 1, 6]. An id is only a label: id 1 is not “more” than id 2

Special tokens

Token What it is for
<unk> Not in the vocabulary: rare or new words
<pad> Filler, so a batch has one length
<bos>, <eos> Start and end of a sequence
  • The vocabulary is fixed from the training text. Shorter sequences are padded to share a batch
  • Names vary (<s> and </s>, [CLS] and [SEP]), the roles do not
  • From here on our sentence is wrapped: <bos> the cat sat on the mat and the cat ran <eos>, 12 tokens

What a choice loses

Choice Before After What is lost
Lowercase everything The cat and the cat the cat Which word began a sentence
Drop punctuation Stop. Go! stop go Where sentences end
Keep only common words a quokka sat a <unk> sat The rare word itself
  • Every preprocessing step throws something away. Whether that matters depends on the task
  • Lowercasing is fine for sorting reviews by topic, and not fine for finding names

Characters, words, or pieces of words?

Token Vocabulary size Sequence length for our sentence
Characters 12 38
Words 7 10
Pieces of words in between in between
  • Characters: a tiny vocabulary, but long sequences. Words: short sequences, but a huge vocabulary and new words keep arriving
  • Pieces of words (subwords) split a rare word into common parts, such as un + happi + ness. Real systems use these
  • The same tradeoff holds for DNA: single letters, or groups of letters called k-mers

Takeaway

Turning a sequence into tokens decides what the model can see and how long its sequences are.

Encoding words

Simple ways to turn tokens into vectors

One-hot vectors

  • A token is one of a fixed list, a category, just like a class label. So we do what we did for class labels: a one-hot vector, with a 1 in the slot of the word and 0 elsewhere. Each column is one token of the sentence
  • Distance between “cat” and “mat”: 1.41. Between “cat” and “the”: 1.41. Every pair of different words is the same distance apart

Bag of words

Text the cat sat on mat and ran
the cat sat on the mat 2 1 1 1 1 0 0
the mat sat on the cat 2 1 1 1 1 0 0
the cat ran 1 1 0 0 0 0 1
  • A bag of words encodes a whole text as one vector of counts, one slot per vocabulary word
  • Any length of text gives a vector of the same size
  • The first two texts get the same vector. The order is gone
  • Good for simple jobs such as sorting by topic. A common tweak, TF-IDF, down-weights words that appear in every text

What these cannot say

No sense of similarity

  • Every pair of different words is equally far apart
  • “cat” is no closer to “mat” than to “the”, and no closer to a word like “kitten”
  • A model gets no help from words that behave alike

No sense of context

  • One-hot: a word is the same vector wherever it appears
  • Bag of words: the order of the words is thrown away
  • “the cat sat on the mat” and “the mat sat on the cat” look the same


Both are fine for some jobs, and neither gives the vectors we want: ones where closeness means something

Embeddings

Learn the vectors

The Big Idea

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps.”

  • J. R. Firth, 1957
  • A word’s meaning shows in the words that appear around it. That is the idea behind word2vec

word2vec: predict the neighbours

  • Slide a window along the text. The word in the middle is the centre word, the words around it are its neighbours
  • Skip-gram: given the centre word, predict each of its neighbours. The vectors learned along the way are the embeddings

Skip-gram pairs

  • Slide the window so every word takes a turn as the centre. Each arc links two words that share a window, giving two pairs: one in each direction
  • Orange: the window we just saw. “mat” as the centre gives mat → on, mat → the, mat → and, mat → the
  • 42 pairs in all. The labels are free, like the same/different pairs last lecture

A word, in terms of its neighbours

  • Count how often each word (row) has each other word (column) within the window: each row is a word in terms of its neighbours
  • A row is as long as the vocabulary. With thousands of words, it is almost all zeros
  • An embedding plays that role in a short vector of d numbers

Where the vectors live: the embedding table

  • Each token is one row of the embedding table E, a matrix of shape (vocabulary size, d), here 9 \times 3. The highlighted row is “cat” (id 1)
  • The numbers are parameters. They start random, and training moves them so each row describes its token by its neighbours

Breakdown: a lookup is a matrix product

  • The 1 picks out row 1 of E and every other row is multiplied by 0, so the product is that row. The layer just looks the row up
  • A long, mostly-zero vector in, a short dense one out: 9 numbers become 3. With 20,000 words and d = 300, 20,000 become 300

The embedding layer in PyTorch

import torch
from torch import nn

emb = nn.Embedding(num_embeddings=9, embedding_dim=3)       # the table E: 9 tokens, 3 dimensions
ids = torch.tensor([[ID[w] for w in SEQ]])                  # (batch, length) = (1, 12)
out = emb(ids)                                              # (batch, length, dim)
print(ids.shape, "->", out.shape)

onehot = nn.functional.one_hot(ids, 9).float()              # (1, 12, 9)
print(torch.allclose(onehot @ emb.weight, out))             # a lookup equals one-hot times the table
torch.Size([1, 12]) -> torch.Size([1, 12, 3])
True
  • Input: ids of shape (batch, length). Output: one vector per id, shape (batch, length, d)
  • emb.weight is the table, an ordinary parameter that backpropagation updates

How do we train the table?

Inside a task

  • Put the table at the front of a model with a task, such as predicting the next word
  • That task’s loss is the training signal
  • The vectors are a by-product

On neighbours alone

  • Train on one question: which words appear near each other?
  • word2vec’s own loss is the training signal
  • The vectors are the product. This is the route we use


Either way: start from random rows, compute a loss, and backpropagation moves the rows of the table

The skip-gram loss

One training example is a centre word with vector \mathbf{u}, one real neighbour \mathbf{v}, and a few random words \mathbf{v_n}.

\text{loss} = \underbrace{-\log \sigma(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})}_{\text{real neighbour}} \;\underbrace{-\sum_n \log \sigma(-\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v_n})}_{\text{random words}}

  • It is word2vec’s own loss. Training makes it small by scoring the real neighbour high and the random words low
  • The same idea as the twin network’s contrastive loss, in a different form: real pairs together, other pairs apart

Breakdown: the skip-gram loss

Piece What it is
\mathbf{u} The centre word’s vector: its row of the table
\mathbf{v} A real neighbour’s vector: a word inside the centre word’s window
\mathbf{v_n} A random word’s vector, drawn from the vocabulary. A few per example, so the sum
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} The score for the pair, read through \sigma as a probability that they are neighbours
-\log \sigma(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) Real-neighbour term: small when the real pair scores high
-\log \sigma(-\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v_n}) Random-word term: the minus sign inside flips it, so it is small when the random word scores low
  • Without the random-word term, every vector could point the same way and the real-neighbour term would be satisfied
  • How it is used: for each (centre, neighbour) pair, raise the score of the two rows, and lower the centre’s score with a few random words (negative samples). Backpropagation moves the rows

The skip-gram loss with real numbers

Pair Dot product The term with the number in Loss
Real neighbour, score high +2 -\log \sigma(2) 0.13
Real neighbour, score low -2 -\log \sigma(-2) 2.13
Random word, score low -2 -\log \sigma(2) 0.13
Random word, score high +2 -\log \sigma(-2) 2.13
  • Because \sigma(2) = 0.88: a real pair that scores +2 costs 0.13, and one that scores -2 costs 2.13
  • For a random word the signs flip. Scoring high costs 2.13, so training lowers that score

A sentence becomes a sequence of vectors

  • Look up each token’s row: the sentence becomes a sequence of vectors, shape (12, 3). This is what the model is given
  • The order is kept, and a repeated word gets the same vector: “the” (orange) three times
  • A batch has shape (batch, length, d), as in the PyTorch slide

One vector

Takeaway

An embedding layer turns ids into vectors, and word2vec trains them so that words used in similar company end up close.

Vector size Similar words close? Built by

Recap: key ideas

  • A sequence is data where order matters, and the basic task is to predict the next item. Items can be numbers, or symbols such as words, letters in DNA, pages, notes
  • Symbols become tokens, then ids. Every choice (case, punctuation, characters or words) throws something away. Special tokens (<unk>, <pad>, <bos>, <eos>) cover the unknown, padding and the ends
  • One-hot and bag of words are simple encodings with no sense of similarity or order
  • An embedding is a learned vector per token. A lookup is a one-hot vector times a table. word2vec trains it with the skip-gram loss on neighbours
